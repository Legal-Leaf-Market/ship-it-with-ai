Case study

Heart of Columbus

A local guide that sends customers straight to independent businesses, built and run by one person with AI coding tools, and held to rules a newsroom would recognize.

The problem

People looking for a barber, a taco or a gift in Columbus mostly land on national listing sites and delivery apps. Those send traffic to whoever pays, take a cut of orders, and are often wrong about hours.

The goal was a guide that sends people to the business itself: its own ordering page, its own booking page, its own shop. No middleman in the checkout, and nothing on the page that the business did not say.

What I built

One page per business

Every listing has its own address, filed under categories and neighborhoods, with structured data built only from what the page shows.

Search that ranks, never hides

Every word must match somewhere, then results are ordered by name, a specific type, the street and finally the category.

Direct order and book links

Buttons go to each business’s own provider page, such as Toast, Square or Vagaro. Marketplaces that take a cut are never labeled as direct.

Cart handoff to the shop’s checkout

Public Shopify, WooCommerce and Squarespace catalogs are read live, and the cart is handed to the shop’s own checkout. A nightly check proves the handoff still works on the real shops.

Live local events

Library and parks calendars are merged live with hand-entered listings, filtered to public events, and exported as a calendar feed, a spreadsheet and newspaper style text.

Storefront demos

Five demo storefronts for local owners, each clearly labeled as a demo that never sends an order, booking or text.

Visitor guides in two languages

Guides for visitors, free things to do and holidays, with Spanish versions that stay marked as under review until a fluent speaker signs off.

A print edition

A 16-page December edition laid out from the same data, plus illustrated listing art.

What I would bring to your project

Rules that keep it trustworthy

  • Every fact has a source. Addresses, phones and hours come from public listings or the business’s own website, with the date they were checked.
  • Nothing is made up. No invented reviews, ratings, owner names or history. Anything unverified is labeled as a sample.
  • Paid placement is always labeled. A paid spot shows its label everywhere it appears, and search ranking never reads it.
  • One policy decides what search engines index. Demos, previews and private pages stay out, and the page tag and the server header always agree.
  • Tests guard the rules. The build fails if a page breaks one of them, so a rule cannot quietly slip.

Running it on a small budget

Every production deploy on the host costs credits. Publishing on every merge used them up in November 2026. I moved publishing to a separate live branch that goes out in batches, as soon as five site changes are waiting or the oldest has waited four hours, with a manual button for urgent fixes. Previews stay free.

It is the kind of fix most small businesses need: find where money leaks quietly, then change the process, not just the bill.
